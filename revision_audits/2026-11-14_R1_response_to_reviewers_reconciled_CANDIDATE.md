Manuscript SIGPRO-D-26-02622: “Robust Masked Signal Modeling under Impulsive Noise: A Fractional Lower-Order Perspective”

We thank the editor and reviewers for their careful assessment and constructive comments. We have revised the manuscript and Supplementary Material in response. Below, we address each comment and identify the corresponding changes by section, figure, or table.

## Reviewer 1

We thank Reviewer 1 for the detailed comments on the theoretical scope, related work, trained-residual evidence, baseline sensitivity, and external evaluation. We respond point by point below.

### Proactive disclosure: correction to the submitted synthetic sampler

During the revision, we found an error in the submitted Chambers–Mallows–Stuck sampler: it used $\cos(U)$ instead of the $\cos((1-\alpha)U)$ factor required by the symmetric stable construction. The affected submitted $\alpha=1.5$ results therefore did not come from the stated noise law. We withdraw the original Table 3 results and Table 4 significance claims; they are not evidence for the revised conclusions.

A separate provenance check also identified an inconsistency between the historical L1 record and a deterministic reproduction of the mathematically identical p=1 objective. We therefore use one explicitly designated, consistently reconciled five-seed run for the core table rather than merge earlier results. We regenerated the affected corrected-CMS experiments and replaced the corresponding manuscript results. In the designated five-seed corrected-CMS $\alpha=1.5$ core benchmark (Table 1), MSE mean accuracy is 93.6/94.5/95.7/97.0%, and the FLOM-based $p$-moment loss ($p=1.2$) is 94.1/96.0/97.0/98.5%, at 50/100/200/500 total labeled examples. Their unrounded differences are +0.44/+1.52/+1.24/+1.44 percentage points. The highest mean is attained by the $p$-moment loss at 50 and 200 labels and by L1 at 100 and 500 labels. These are descriptive differences rather than statistically established advantages: each loss has one pre-trained encoder evaluated with five matched downstream fine-tuning seeds, and the minimum attainable exact two-sided sign-flip $p$-value is 0.0625. The revised experiment is not a paired rerun of the original, so the full numerical change cannot be attributed solely to the sampler correction. Seven deterministic sampler tests now cover reproducibility, symmetry, the $\alpha=1$ and Gaussian limits, characteristic-function agreement, input validation, and distinction from the legacy transform.

The sampler error does not affect $\alpha=1$; CWRU uses a separate CMS implementation, and NOISEX uses recorded noise. The ten-run $\alpha=1$ comparison uses independent random streams, so we replaced paired tests with Welch comparisons. The revision-specific corrected code and configurations are provided as a separate reproducibility archive; the original public repository is a legacy record and is not the source for the revised results.

### R1.1 Limited methodological novelty

> The paper applies established robust and fractional lower-order losses to masked self-supervised reconstruction. Proposition 1 and Corollaries 1–2 follow directly from the standard moment condition for symmetric α-stable variables, together with differentiation of (|r|^p) and, for Corollary 2, a bounded-Jacobian assumption. The theoretical development is therefore primarily an application of known results to this setting. The main novelty lies in the problem formulation and the empirical comparison across noise and label regimes. The authors should clarify this scope and avoid overstating the theoretical contribution.

**Response.** We agree that the moment condition and its direct consequences are established results, not new stable-distribution theory. We now state this explicitly, cite Nolan (2020, Sec. 1.5, pp. 16–17), retain the assumptions and signal-processing interpretation in the main text, and place the short derivations in Supplementary Note S12. The paper's contribution is instead a controlled study of a practical design question in masked signal learning: whether lower-order residual scores translate into better downstream performance or smaller network gradients under impulsive corruption. Our results show why that inference cannot be made from the injected-noise tail index alone: the corrected core benchmark has $p$-moment ($p=1.2$) versus MSE mean differences of 0.4–1.5 percentage points, without a statistically established five-seed advantage. A separate joint corruption-and-scaling audit shows within-audit accuracy contrasts of 1.9–3.6 points, but different operation orders and effective noise levels prevent attributing these contrasts to normalization alone or comparing them causally with the core effects. Residual-score concentration also does not order the measured raw parameter-gradient norms. These are protocol-specific empirical findings, not a new general theorem, a causal claim about normalization, or evidence of a universal $p$-moment-loss advantage. The Abstract, Introduction, Discussion, and Conclusion now consistently present this calibrated contribution.

**Location:** Abstract and Introduction (main pp. 1–3); theory (pp. 8–10); empirical residual and gradient diagnostics (pp. 14–17); Discussion and Conclusion (pp. 22–23); Supplementary Note S12.

### R1.2 Incomplete coverage of related work

> Section 2.3 omits several relevant studies on lower-order processing under heavy-tailed or α-stable noise, including [1-4]. These studies examine FLOC-based or (p)-order criteria and their order-selection, stability, and convergence properties. The authors should clarify how the proposed (|r|^p) reconstruction loss differs from these formulations.
>
> In addition, the authors should distinguish fractional lower-order statistics from fractional-order neural dynamics. The latter have been used for robust and multiscale graph representation learning [5–7], but rely on fractional derivatives rather than lower-order moments. A brief discussion would clarify this distinction and better position the proposed FLOC reconstruction loss. The discussion in Section 2.1 should also cover recent noise-aware self-supervised methods. For example, [8] mitigates representation noise through complementary feature and structural views. Although it does not employ fractional dynamics or lower-order statistics, it provides a relevant contrast to the loss-level robustness studied in this paper.
>
> [1] Zulawunski et al, Fractional lower-order covariance-based measures for cyclostationary time series with heavy-tailed distributions: application to dependence testing and model order identification, 2026.
>
> [2] Feng et al, An active noise control algorithm based on fractional lower order covariance with on-line characteristics estimation, 2023.
>
> [3] Feng et al, Projection FxLMS Framework of Active Noise Control Against Impulsive Noise Environments, 2024.
>
> [4] Luo et al, Adaptive filtering algorithm based on Beta fluctuation and fractional order differential evolution in impulsive noise, 2025.
>
> [5] Kang et al, Coupling Graph Neural Networks with Fractional Order Continuous Dynamics: A Robustness Study, AAAI 2025.
>
> [6] Zhao et al, Simple Graph Contrastive Learning via Fractional-order Neural Diffusion Networks, 2025.
>
> [7] Zhao et al, Adaptive Multi-view Graph Contrastive Learning via Fractional-order Neural Diffusion Networks, 2025.
>
> [8] Zhao et al, Less is More: Towards Simple Graph Contrastive Learning, 2025.

**Response.** We agree that the original discussion did not adequately separate these mechanisms. The revision now distinguishes three different mathematical objects and intervention levels. First, the cited FLOS/FLOC signal-processing studies [1–4] use lower-order covariance or related $p$-order criteria for statistical characterization, dependence/model-order analysis, filtering (including FxLMS), or an optimizer update; our $|r|^p$ is a pointwise loss on masked-reconstruction residuals, not a lower-order covariance estimator or an adaptive-filtering algorithm. Second, the fractional-order graph neural networks [5–7] use fractional derivatives/continuous dynamics to change internal information propagation and graph representations. They are architectural or representation-dynamics methods, not fractional lower-order residual statistics, and their robustness results do not by themselves imply robustness of our loss. Third, Zhao et al.'s Less is More [8] combines feature and structural graph views in a contrastive representation-learning objective to mitigate feature noise; it is a representation-level noise-aware method, not a pointwise $p$-moment masked-reconstruction objective or a masked signal-reconstruction baseline. These distinctions explain why the shared word “fractional” does not indicate a shared mathematical mechanism, and why we cite these works for positioning rather than claim direct experimental superiority over them.

The revised Related Work also covers the signal/time-series masked-learning context, robust-loss comparators, and two direct noisy masked-learning neighbors: UNet-INSN uses global-mask self-supervised denoising for power-line communication, whereas Bedir et al. predict discrete symbol identifiers under impulsive-channel corruption for demodulation. Those methods target denoising or symbol classification, respectively; our study isolates the reconstruction-loss choice within masked signal pretraining. We make no priority claim for masked learning under noise and do not claim lower-order robust estimation itself as new. The broader modality/application survey, including graph-fractional and FLOS/impulsive-noise work, is retained in Supplementary Note S1 with its own bibliography.

**Location:** Related Work (main pp. 4–5); Supplementary Note S1.

### R1.3 Insufficient empirical validation of the residual model

> Proposition 1 and Corollaries 1–2 assume that the reconstruction residual follows a symmetric α-stable distribution. During training, however, the residual is determined jointly by the normalized input and the learned finite-capacity autoencoder; its distribution may therefore deviate from the assumed model and change across training stages. The diagnostic in Section 3.2 samples directly from an α-stable distribution and only illustrates the theoretical assumption. It does not establish that actual training residuals exhibit the same tail behavior. The authors should examine the residual distributions and gradient-tail statistics at representative training stages and assess whether the condition in Equation (11) predicts the observed behavior. Without such validation, the theory provides only limited support for the empirical findings.

**Response.** We agree that an idealized stable-residual simulation cannot establish the distribution of residuals learned by the network. Our revised response is threefold. First, the primary protocol applies per-sequence max-absolute normalization to length-128 inputs. At any fixed finite-parameter checkpoint, the continuous network map is bounded on this compact input domain for each of the finitely many masks; consequently, the reconstruction residual is bounded and cannot literally follow a non-degenerate $\alpha$-stable law for $\alpha<2$. The stable moment condition is therefore a conditional reference, not a literal residual model for this normalized experiment.

Second, we added held-out residual, score, and gradient diagnostics at epochs 1, 5, 10, and 20 over five seeds. These show that residual-score concentration does not predict the ordering of total network-gradient norms. For example, the largest 0.1% of squared score mass is 0.278% for the $p$-moment loss ($p=1.2$), compared with 3.182% for MSE, yet this ordering does not carry over to the measured parameter-gradient norms. The held-out gradients are recomputed before clipping; the checkpoints were learned with Adam and encoder-only global-norm clipping at 1.0. Supplementary Table S15 reports the median, $q_{.90}$, and maximum of eight held-out batch-total gradient norms per seed and stage. These are batch-level summaries, not coordinatewise gradient-tail estimates. The stagewise diagnostics use independently sampled Bernoulli(0.5) token masks rather than the core benchmark's fixed 16-of-32 masks, and compare raw gradients from different trained checkpoints under the stated loss scalings.

Third, we now define the scope of the theory more narrowly: it describes pointwise loss-score behavior under an idealized heavy-tailed residual model, while our diagnostics test the limits of that mechanism under the stated normalized training protocol. For $p=1.2$, the $p$-moment-loss and MSE score magnitudes cross at $|r|=0.5281$; below this value the $p$-moment loss relatively amplifies nonzero residuals, and above it attenuates them. Supplementary Note S17 proves the fixed-residual top-$k$ score-share result and gives a bounded Jacobian-cancellation example, explaining why residual-score behavior alone cannot order full network gradients. Our finite-sample diagnostics do not establish a population residual law, validate Equation (11) as a general predictor, or provide a convergence guarantee.

**Location:** Trained residuals, gradient diagnostics, score crossover, and local slices (main pp. 14–17); compact-support implication (Discussion, pp. 22–23); Supplementary Table S15, Figs. S7–S8, Notes S12/S14/S17 and Table S14.

## Reviewer 3

### General assessment

> This article addresses a key signal processing design choice for self-supervised learning. For impulsive alpha-stable noise, a reconstruction loss function based on the fractional lower-order moment is proposed. A thorough comparison with several key loss functions is conducted on both synthetic and real datasets. Further, the effectiveness of the proposed method is demonstrated through downstream classification tasks. While the topic of the manuscript is highly relevant and the experiments are well thought-out, in my opinion, the authors should address several key issues before it can be considered for publication. I, therefore, recommend a major revision of the manuscript. The comments are detailed below:

We thank the reviewer for the positive assessment and for the specific requests concerning optimization diagnostics, masking, architecture, workflow clarity, and noise-family coverage. We respond to each item below.

### R3.1 Training gradients and loss landscape

> The parameter gradient moment is shown to be theoretically bounded. But how do the gradients of the corresponding loss functions behave during training? A detailed loss landscape analysis is missing.

**Response.** We agree that the original theory did not describe training-stage behavior. We added stage-wise residual-score and parameter-gradient diagnostics, three-seed pretraining trajectories, and local loss slices over five directions and three seeds (60 curves across four losses). In all 60 sampled slices, the held-out objective increased monotonically with distance from the trained point across the five sampled relative radii. The median increase averaged over the negative and positive endpoints was 3.15% (range 2.10–4.89% across slices); separate endpoint medians were 3.29% and 3.06%. These are descriptive summaries of sampled directions, not estimates of global curvature. The observed network-gradient ordering does not follow the residual-score concentration ordering. We therefore limit the interpretation to finite-sample diagnostics and sampled local geometry. The stagewise and local-slice diagnostics use Bernoulli(0.5) token masks, not the exact-count masks of Table 1. Raw gradient norms are loss-scale dependent and the models have different residuals and Jacobians, so no common-checkpoint counterfactual or isolated Jacobian mechanism has been established. We have not performed a global loss-landscape or Hessian analysis and do not claim convergence or optimizer stability.

The added score-crossover analysis and bounded Jacobian-cancellation example (Supplementary Note S17) also clarify why residual-score concentration need not rank the full parameter gradient.

**Location:** Residual/score/gradient diagnostics and local loss slices (main pp. 14–17); Supplementary Note S17, Fig. S1, and Figs. S7–S8.

### R3.2 p–α practice and convergence assumptions

> In theory, the parameter p is dependent on the tail index α, but in practice, p is chosen somewhat heuristically. It may be hard to determine the tail index of the residuals from the corrupted signal. This brings back the first point: how will the convergence properties for this specific optimization problem change when the theoretical assumptions and guarantees are not strictly maintained in practice?

**Response.** We agree that the injected-noise tail index does not determine the learned residual law and that the original presentation could suggest a stronger theory-to-optimization link than is justified. The revision distinguishes the finite-objective condition `p < α` from the residual-score moment condition `q(p−1) < α`, states that the residual itself must satisfy the idealized law for those conclusions to apply, and adds empirical trained-residual diagnostics. We further conducted an exploratory Hill-estimator threshold-sensitivity check on reconstructable archived residual samples. Supplementary Table S14 reports median estimates across five seeds at displayed thresholds $k=100,1{,}000,5{,}000,10{,}000$: MSE 12.4, 7.4, 3.2, and 2.6; Charbonnier 11.5, 8.5, 3.3, and 2.6; $p$-moment loss ($p=1.2$) 11.9, 8.1, 3.2, and 2.5. The strong threshold dependence provides no stable plateau for selecting a residual $\alpha$. Dependence among coordinates and five training seeds also preclude interpreting the seed ranges as iid confidence intervals. We therefore retain validation-based loss selection and do not claim an effective stable-law index. We extended the bounded-Jacobian moment argument to a finite-coordinate averaged reconstruction objective, while keeping the attenuation comparison coordinatewise and explicitly avoiding a claim about summed network-gradient ordering. The revised Discussion clarifies that violating an idealized population-moment condition does not itself imply divergence of a finite training run, and that a finite empirical trajectory does not establish population convergence or stationarity. Section 4.3 now states that the threshold-sensitive Hill diagnostic has no stable plateau, so we do not estimate a residual tail index to select $p$. We treat $p=1.2$ as a tested candidate, not an order inferred from the injected-noise index. The Discussion and Conclusion separately state that our diagnostics do not yield a universal loss selector or convergence guarantee. We do not derive convergence rates or guarantees under assumption mismatch. In the targeted ten-run Cauchy-level experiment, each loss used a distinct random-stream offset for pre-training and downstream data, masks, and initialization; equal run indices are not paired. We therefore replaced the paired tests with independent-sample Welch tests for the $p$-moment loss ($p=1.2$) versus MSE: raw $p$-values at 50/100/200/500 labels are $1.09\times10^{-5}$, $4.41\times10^{-7}$, $2.62\times10^{-8}$, and $2.47\times10^{-5}$, remaining below 0.001 after Holm correction across those four comparisons. The corresponding L1 and Charbonnier means are similar or higher at some budgets; these are descriptive comparisons, and we make no paired-seed or equivalence claim. The complete mean±SD table is Supplementary Table S13, and Supplementary Note S18 documents the RNG structure and tests. A separate five-seed dynamics diagnostic records fixed-held-out masked objective and batch-total gradient norms during the matched 20-epoch $\alpha=1$ pretraining schedule: the held-out objective falls in all 20 seed--loss runs, the batch-gradient $q_{.90}$ falls from epoch 1 to 20 in all 20, and no non-finite update occurs. Median held-out reductions range from 70.1% to 76.0% across the four losses (Supplementary Note S16 and Table S16). These are finite-run observations under the tested setup; loss scales are not compared across families. Because $p=1.2>\alpha=1$, the Cauchy result fails the finite-risk criterion only under the idealized unnormalized S$\alpha$S residual model; the finite-sequence max-normalized experiment itself has bounded residuals and finite empirical objective. **We still do not derive a convergence rate or guarantee for the mismatched, finite-sample neural optimization problem. The reviewer’s general convergence question therefore remains theoretically unanswered and is an explicit limitation.**

**Location:** Stable-residual theory (main pp. 8–10), trained-residual and optimization diagnostics (pp. 14–17), Student-$t$ and multi-$\alpha$ stress checks (p. 21), and Discussion/Conclusion (pp. 22–23); Supplementary Notes S12/S14/S16/S18 and Tables S13/S14/S16/S17.

### R3.3 Masking strategy

> Analysis of the dependence of the training loss on the masking strategy is missing.

**Response.** We agree and added a direct comparison between exact-count random-token masking and contiguous-block masking at the same 50% token-mask ratio, alongside a two-endpoint mask-ratio sensitivity analysis. The strategy comparison covers four losses, 100- and 200-label settings, and five downstream fine-tuning seeds, with pretraining-objective curves; random-token masking has higher mean accuracy in seven of eight displayed loss–budget cells and ties the remaining $p$-moment/200-label cell at displayed precision; the differences range from 0.00 to 1.60 percentage points. We now clarify an important architectural limitation: masking is applied after a Conv1D tokenizer with overlapping receptive fields (kernel 8, stride 4, padding 2), so the 50% ratio is a token ratio, not strict concealment of half the raw samples; the reconstruction loss is evaluated over the full signal. We therefore interpret the random-token/block comparison as a comparison of token-mask patterns under this overlapping tokenizer, not an isolated raw-sample concealment effect. The loss covers all raw-sample positions, unlike canonical masked-patch-only MAE; this study does not include a full-signal-versus-masked-only objective ablation. A separate family-aligned randomized-parameter task also lowers accuracy and gives mixed rankings (Supplementary Table S17), but it is not a masking ablation. The ratio audit compares only the provenance-verified 0.25 and 0.75 settings: the 0.75 setting is 3.7–6.0 points lower at 50 labels, 1.0–2.3 points lower at 200 labels, and 0.2–0.4 points lower at 500 labels. We do not infer an optimum or an intermediate-ratio trend. Each loss/strategy condition has only one pretrained model, so downstream-seed standard deviations do not estimate pretraining variability; we keep the findings descriptive. The exact historical random-token masking runner was not recovered; the public frozen archive retains its configuration and output records but cannot provide a complete source-level rerun of that one arm. This limitation is documented in the release README.

**Location:** Masking sensitivity and strategy comparison (main pp. 19–20); Supplementary Tables S4–S5 and Fig. S2.

### R3.4 Architecture rationale

> How is the model architecture chosen for this specific problem? Did the authors draw inspiration from any prior literature? It would be helpful to clarify this point.

**Response.** We revised the Methods to make the design lineage explicit: the pipeline follows the established masked-autoencoding pattern used in vision and time-series representation learning, while using a Conv1D tokenizer for raw signal segments and a Transformer encoder for contextual modeling. We cite the masked-autoencoder, Ti-MAE, and Transformer foundations at that point. Unlike canonical MAE's visible-patch-only encoding and masked-patch reconstruction objective, our method inserts learned mask tokens after overlapping Conv1D tokenization and reconstructs the full observed signal. We do not claim a masked-only-target ablation. The specific tokenizer and compact four-layer, 64-dimensional, four-head configuration are implementation choices, not architecture-search results. We hold this backbone fixed across losses to isolate reconstruction-objective effects; we do not claim architectural optimality or a universal prescription. The workflow diagram depicts a generic “few-label” fine-tuning stage; the accompanying protocol distinguishes the primary synthetic 50–500 total-label budgets from external task-specific shot budgets.

**Location:** Model/objective setup (main pp. 6–8), architecture and experimental protocol (pp. 11–12); Supplementary Table S8.
